Chapter One

Far out along the dusty, unfashionable edge of the Orion Spur, there is a small yellow sun that nobody has ever bothered to name. Around it, at a distance of roughly ninety-three million miles, turns a damp blue planet whose inhabitants are still very pleased that they invented the toaster.

Most of them are, by any reasonable standard, quite unhappy. This is not because the toaster was a bad idea. It is because the toaster was followed by the spreadsheet, the meeting, and the meeting about the spreadsheet, none of which had been asked for by anybody.

On a grey Thursday in late October, a man called Desmond Pryce woke up at 7:42 and decided, with the calm certainty of someone who has not yet looked out of the window, that today would be an ordinary day.

He was wrong. He was, in fact, about as wrong as it is possible for a man in striped pyjamas to be.

The bulldozer that was parked on his front lawn had been sent by the Wexford County Council, which had approved the new bypass in 1987 and had then forgotten to tell anyone. The plans were displayed in a filing cabinet in a locked basement in Leeds. The basement was guarded by a sign that said "Beware of the Leopard", although nobody could remember whether there had ever actually been a leopard.

"You can't just knock my house down," said Desmond, who was standing in the mud in his slippers.

"I can, actually," said the foreman, a large and patient man named Gordon Oyelaran, who had spent twenty-two years knocking things down and had found that people almost always said exactly this. "It's all been signed. You were notified."

"I was not notified."

"You were notified in the the usual way."

Desmond lay down in front of the bulldozer. It was a brave gesture, and it was also rather cold.

Meanwhile, some four hundred and twelve light years away, a vast grey ship that had been built by a committee, and which looked very much like it, was drifting slowly towards the Earth, carrying a set of plans of its own, which had been displayed for fifty years in a planning office on Alpha Centauri that, due to the fact that it was located at the bottom of a particularly unpleasant gravity well and was open only on alternate Tuesdays between the hours of two and three in the afternoon, had basically never been visited by a single member of the public who was in any way likely to object.

Nobody on Earth knew this. If they had known, they would probably have made a cup of tea about it.

Desmond's oldest friend, a quiet woman called Ingrid Halvorsen, arrived at ten o'clock with a carrier bag and a look of extreme urgency.

"Get up," she said. "We need to go to the pub."

"I'm protesting."

"Desmond, I am really, truly serious. How much do you care about your house?"

He thought about it. The house had a leaking roof, a temperamental boiler and a kitchen that smelled faintly of onions no matter what was cooked in it.

"Quite a lot," he said.

"Now," said Ingrid, "how much do you care about the planet?"
